Avian sex chromosomes (Z, W) were considered to have remained relatively stable over more than 100 million years of evolution. The availability of contiguous genomes in recent years, however, has enabled the identification of many sex chromosomal fusions with autosomes, forming neo-sex chromosomes. Once the autosomal portion gets fused, it behaves like a sex chromosome and undergoes recombination suppression. Despite recent findings, the phylogenetic extent and the temporal dynamics of these fusions in birds remain poorly understood.	

Using chromosome-level assemblies and gene annotations from the Vertebrate Genomes Project (VGP), we searched for species with enlarged Z chromosomes to identify previously undescribed neo-sex fusions. We also assessed recombination suppression in clades with neo-sex chromosomes by phylogenetic clustering of Z and W homologs and looked for functional enrichment with a Gene Ontology (GO) analysis. We then built a hierarchical Bayesian model based on the expected shift in synonymous substitution rate (dS) after sex-linkage to estimate fusion age.

We identified six bird species with previously undescribed neo-sex chromosomes, plus additional independent fusions in two Psittaciformes species. Parrots and Myzomela (Meliphagidae) species show complete recombination suppression over the fused region, whereas ibises (Threskiornithidae) retain a neo-pseudoautosomal region at the distal end and Sylvioidea passerines show signs of lineage-specific suppression. In Sylvioidea, synonymous substitutions in the fused region accumulate non-neutrally, which may reflect GC-biased gene conversion. Some neo-sex chromosomes were enriched for particular functions, but none with clear links to reproduction. Applied to Psittaciformes, our model estimated a fusion age of about 44–51 MYA (95% CI across calibrations: 39–55 MYA). Overall, our results enable the investigation of temporal dynamics that determine sex chromosome evolution by using neo-sex chromosomes of different ages.
